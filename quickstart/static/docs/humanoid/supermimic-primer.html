Primer for newcomers · humanoid whole-body control

SuperMimic, world models, and training humanoids

Four parts, read in order. The diagrams carry most of the explanation, and every equation has a worked number next to it.

The paper

SuperMimic learns a differentiable copy of the physics simulator (a world model) and backpropagates through it to train one controller that copies 129,514 human motions. 3 hours on 1 GPU beats a baseline trained for 7 days on 128 GPUs. The result runs on a real Unitree G1.

World model

A neural network that predicts the next state from the current state and action, \(\hat s_{t+1} = f(s_t, a_t)\). You can train a policy inside it instead of inside the simulator.

Before you start

Learn the control loop (RL, PD control), body math (frames, gravity, contacts), reward and termination design, domain randomization, and hardware safety.

Part 1 · Basics

How a robot learns a skill

Reinforcement learning (RL): a policy picks actions, the world answers, and a score (reward) says how well it went. Training changes the policy's weights to raise the total score.

The policy and what it maximizes
\[ a_t = \pi_\theta(o_t,\, g_t) \qquad\qquad J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t\ge 0} \gamma^t\, r_{t+1}\Big] \]
  • \(o_t\) observationwhat the sensors read at step \(t\)
  • \(g_t\) goalthe target, here the next frames of a human motion
  • \(a_t\) actionwhat the policy commands
  • \(r_{t+1}\) rewardthe score for that one step
  • \(\pi_\theta\) policya neural network with weights \(\theta\)
  • \(\gamma\) discounthow much later rewards count (0.99)
  • \(J(\theta)\) returntotal discounted reward; training pushes it up
With \(\gamma = 0.99\), a reward 100 steps (2 s) ahead counts \(0.99^{100} \approx 0.37\) as much as one now. So the policy mostly cares about the next second or two.

Motion tracking, the “mimic” in SuperMimic

The goal is a reference motion: a human motion-capture clip already mapped onto the robot's joints. The reward is high when each robot body part is where the reference says it should be.

Body-position reward · one of six tracking terms
\[ r_{\text{pos}} = \exp\!\Big(-\frac{1}{|\mathcal T|}\sum_{b\in\mathcal T}\frac{\lVert p_b^{\text{des}} - p_b\rVert^2}{\sigma^2}\Big), \qquad \sigma = 0.3\ \text{m} \]

\(p_b^{\text{des}}\) = where body part \(b\) should be, \(p_b\) = where it is, \(\mathcal T\) = the tracked body parts. The full reward adds the same shape for orientation, linear velocity and angular velocity of the bodies and the root, then subtracts two penalties: jerky actions \(\lVert a_t - a_{t-1}\rVert^2\) and joint-limit violations.

Average error 0.1 m → \(e^{-0.11} = 0.89\). Error 0.3 m → \(e^{-1} = 0.37\). Error 0.6 m → \(e^{-4} = 0.02\).

From action to motor torque

The policy does not output torques. It outputs a joint target, and a fast PD controller (proportional-derivative: push toward the target, brake against speed) turns that target into torque.

Action → target → torque
\[ q^{\text{sp}} = \alpha\, a_t + q^0, \qquad\qquad \tau = K_p\,(q^{\text{sp}} - q) - K_d\,\dot q \]
\(K_p = 40\) N·m/rad, \(K_d = 2\) N·m·s/rad. Target 0.50 rad, joint at 0.40 rad moving at 1 rad/s: \(\tau = 40 \cdot 0.10 - 2 \cdot 1 = 2\) N·m. With low gains like these the joint never quite reaches the target, so the target behaves more like a torque command. The paper uses low gains on purpose.
Part 2 · World models

A learned simulator you can differentiate

A world model is a neural network trained to predict what happens next. Once trained, you can run the robot “in imagination” on the GPU, without calling the simulator.

Predict the next state · and how it is trained
\[ \hat s_{t+1} = f_\omega(s_t,\, a_t) \qquad\qquad \mathcal L(\omega) = \big\lVert f_\omega(s_t, a_t) - s_{t+1} \big\rVert^2 \]

The hat \(\hat{\ }\) marks a prediction. \(\omega\) are the model's weights. \(s_{t+1}\) is the true next state, logged from the simulator.

The simulator says the knee moved from 0.40 to 0.45 rad. The model predicts 0.43. Error 0.02 rad, loss \(0.02^2 = 0.0004\). Gradient descent on \(\omega\) shrinks this over millions of logged steps.

The catch: errors stack up

Feed the model its own predictions and small errors grow. SuperMimic uses two fixes: train the model on 8-step chains of its own predictions, and imagine only 32 steps ahead, letting a critic (a network that estimates future return) cover the rest.

Two ways to learn from a world model

A · likelihood-ratio gradient (PPO-style)
\[ \nabla_\theta J \approx \mathbb{E}\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid o_t)\; A_t\big] \]

\(A_t\) = advantage: how much better the sampled action did than average. Many noisy samples are needed to average out the noise.

B · analytic gradient (BPTT through \(f_\omega\))
\[ \frac{\partial \hat s_{t+1}}{\partial \theta} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial f}{\partial \hat s_t}\,\frac{\partial \hat s_t}{\partial \theta}}_{\text{through the past state}} + \underbrace{\frac{\partial f}{\partial a_t}\,\frac{\partial a_t}{\partial \theta}}_{\text{through this action}} \]

Apply this at every step and sum the reward gradients. Lower variance over short horizons, but it needs a model that is smooth and accurate enough to differentiate.

Toy world \(s_{t+1} = s_t + a_t\), start \(s_0 = 0\), policy \(a_t = \theta\), reward \(-(s_2 - 1)^2\) after two steps. Then \(s_2 = 2\theta\), \(J = -(2\theta - 1)^2\), and \(dJ/d\theta = -4(2\theta - 1)\). At \(\theta = 0\) the gradient is +4: raise \(\theta\). At \(\theta = 0.5\) it is 0: done. No sampling needed.

Part 3 · The paper

SuperMimic: one world model for every motion

Liao, Shao, Long, Zakka, Horowitz, Abbeel, Sreenath · UC Berkeley · CoRL 2026 (oral) · arXiv 2610.28480 · project page with videos

Problem. Tracking a big motion library with PPO is expensive: SONIC used 128 GPUs for 7 days. Idea. Every clip is performed by the same body under the same physics, so learn that physics once, as one world model, and reuse it for all 129,514 clips.

Step 2 · Fit the world model

The model has four heads: next state, reward, observation, and “did it fall”. It also takes \(\xi\), the random physics settings of that episode, so the model can stay deterministic.

The four heads
\[ \hat s_{t+1}=f_\omega(\hat s_t,a_t,\xi) \quad \hat r_{t+1}=r_\omega(\hat s_t,a_t,g_t) \quad \hat{\bar o}_t=g_\omega(\hat s_t,\xi) \quad \hat d_t=d_\omega(\hat s_t,g_t) \]

\(\bar o_t\) = observation without sensor noise. \(d_t\) = 1 if the robot fell (episode ends). Heads that can be computed exactly from the state are computed, not learned.

Eq. 1 · world-model loss
\[ \mathcal L_{\text{WM}}(\omega)=\mathbb E_{\mathcal B}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Big[\underbrace{\lVert\hat s_j-s_j\rVert^2}_{\text{state}}+\underbrace{(\hat r_j-r_j)^2}_{\text{reward}}+\underbrace{\lVert\hat{\bar o}_j-\bar o_j\rVert^2}_{\text{observation}}+\underbrace{\mathrm{BCE}(\hat d_j,d_j)}_{\text{fell or not}}\Big] \]

\(k = 8\). Start from a real logged state \(\hat s_0 = s_0\), feed the logged actions, and let the model use its own predictions for 8 steps. BCE (binary cross-entropy) is the standard loss for yes/no labels.

Step 3 · Improve the policy through the model

Eq. 2 · policy objective
\[ \hat J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{s_0\sim\rho_0,\,\pi_\theta}\Big[\underbrace{\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\gamma^t\,\hat c_t\,\hat r_{t+1}}_{\text{rewards for 32 imagined steps}}+\underbrace{\gamma^H\,\hat c_H\,V_\psi(\hat s_H,g_H,\xi)}_{\text{critic: everything after}}\Big] \] \[ \hat c_0 = 1, \qquad \hat c_{t+1} = \hat c_t\,\big(1-\hat d_{t+1}\big) \qquad\qquad a_t = \pi_\theta(\hat{\bar o}_t + \eta_t,\; g_t) \]

\(\rho_0\) = start states sampled from the replay buffer. \(\hat c_t\) = soft “still standing” weight. \(\eta_t\) = sensor noise added back so the policy stays used to noisy sensors.

If the model predicts a 10% fall chance at each of three steps, \(\hat c\) goes 1 → 0.9 → 0.81 → 0.73. Rewards after a likely fall count less. A hard 0/1 “fell” flag would block the gradient; the soft weight lets it through.
Eq. 3 · critic loss (λ-return targets)
\[ \mathcal L_V(\psi)=\mathbb E\sum_{t=0}^{H-1}\big(V_t-\hat R_t\big)^2, \qquad \hat R_t=\hat r_{t+1}+\gamma\,(1-\hat d_{t+1})\big[(1-\lambda)V_{t+1}+\lambda\hat R_{t+1}\big] \]

\(\hat R_t\) blends “trust the critic now” and “take one more imagined reward, then recurse”. Start from \(\hat R_H = V_H\). \(\lambda = 0.95\), \(\gamma = 0.99\).

Why the critic matters: 32 steps is only 0.64 s. Without a critic, training fails at H ≤ 16 and the policy never settles after a push. With it, H = 16 already works, and the robot recovers from 0.5–4 m/s pushes within 0.5–2.4 s in simulation.

Controlled test (LAFAN1, 40 clips, 2.45 h): SuperMimic reaches return 0.82 in 1.2 h on 1 GPU. PPO in the simulator, with the same reward and networks, never reaches it in 18 h and ends at 0.72 even on 8 GPUs.

Why it works, per the paper's own ablations:

  • One model covers all motions. Every clip is the same body, so more clips add evidence, not new things to learn.
  • Imagination is cheap. An MLP step is over 10× faster than a physics step.
  • Gradients are exact through the model. BPTT needs far fewer samples than PPO's trial-and-score.

Real robot. One policy, no per-motion tuning, on a Unitree G1: a skipping jump, a side roll on the floor, a 90° flip and an in-place 360° flip.

Limits. Flat ground only (no stairs, no objects). The world model learns only from simulator data, never from the real robot. Training several goal types together (for example velocity commands plus motion tracking) is only lightly tested.

Part 4 · Before you start

What to know before you train a humanoid

Learn these, in this order

  1. RL basics and PPO

    Most humanoid work still trains with PPO. Know the clipped objective; it is the baseline every new method compares against.

    \[ L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta)=\mathbb E\Big[\min\big(\rho_t A_t,\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho_t,\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon)\,A_t\big)\Big], \qquad \rho_t=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid o_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t\mid o_t)} \]
    With \(\epsilon = 0.2\): once a good action's probability has grown 20% (\(\rho_t = 1.2\)), the objective stops rewarding further growth in this update. That keeps each step small and stable.

  2. Floating-base dynamics

    \[ M(q)\,\ddot q + h(q,\dot q) = S^\top \tau + J_c^\top f_c \]

    Mass × acceleration (left) = motor torques + contact forces (right). \(S^\top\) maps the 29 motors onto the 35 degrees of freedom; the 6 base ones get nothing. The only way to move the body is to push on the ground, which is why balance is hard.

  3. Rotations and frames

    Policies see the world in the robot's own frame. The most common input is projected gravity: the gravity direction expressed in the pelvis frame. It tells the robot how tilted it is.

  4. Actions and PD control

    Know what your action means: torque, joint target, or joint-target offset, and what the PD gains are. The same policy output means different things under different gains (see Fig 1.3).

  5. Reward and termination design

    Use exponential tracking terms (Fig 1.2) plus small penalties. Then watch for reward hacking, the policy scoring well in an unintended way. Example from the paper: a noisy PPO policy learned to press its own limbs together to damp its action noise, so earlier work added a self-collision penalty. SuperMimic's imagined rollouts are noise-free, so it drops that penalty.

    \[ \text{end episode if}\quad |e_{p,z}| > 0.25\ \text{m} \quad\text{or}\quad e_{\text{tilt}} > 0.8 \]

    \(e_{p,z}\) = root height error vs the reference; \(e_{\text{tilt}}\) = gravity z-part difference (Fig 4.3).

  6. Domain randomization and sim-to-real

    The simulator is never exactly the real robot. Train across many slightly different physics settings so the real robot looks like one more sample.

    \[ \max_\theta\ \mathbb E_{\xi\sim p(\xi)}\big[\,J(\theta;\,\xi)\,\big] \]
    RandomizedRange in the paper
    Foot friction μuniform 0.3 to 1.2
    Joint calibration offset±0.01 rad per joint
    Torso mass-center offset±2.5 cm forward/back, ±5 cm sideways and up/down
    Sensor noisejoint angle ±0.01 rad, joint speed ±0.5 rad/s, gyro ±0.2 rad/s, gravity ±0.05

  7. Hardware safety

    General practice, not from the paper: start on a gantry or harness, keep the emergency stop in hand, test the policy in a second simulator first, begin with slow motions, and log every run so you can replay failures.

Math you need

  • Linear algebra: vectors, matrices, Jacobians
  • Calculus: the chain rule (it is backprop)
  • Probability: expectation, Gaussian noise
  • Rotations: quaternions, frames

Tools

  • Python with PyTorch or JAX
  • GPU simulator: MuJoCo / MuJoCo Warp (used in the paper) or Isaac Lab
  • Motion data: LAFAN1, AMASS, BONES-SEED
  • Your robot's model file (MJCF or URDF)

Reference

Glossary

DoF
degree of freedom: one independent way to move. G1 has 29 motor DoF plus 6 for the free-floating pelvis.
Mocap
motion capture: recorded human movement.
Retargeting
converting a human motion to the robot's joints and limb lengths.
Rollout
running the policy forward for several steps, in the simulator or in imagination.
Replay buffer
a store of recent simulator steps used to train the world model and to seed imagined rollouts.
BPTT
backpropagation through time: the chain rule applied across a sequence of steps.
Critic
a network that estimates the future return from a state.
λ-return / GAE
a training target for the critic that mixes short and long lookahead.
PD gains
\(K_p\) (stiffness) and \(K_d\) (damping) of the joint controller.
Projected gravity
gravity's direction in the robot's own frame; a cheap tilt sensor.
Domain randomization
randomly varying physics in training so the policy works on the real robot.
Sim-to-real
moving a policy trained in simulation onto hardware.
POMDP
partially observable Markov decision process: the formal RL setup where the policy sees only observations, not the full state.
Source: Q. Liao, Y. Shao, J. Long, K. Zakka, R. Horowitz, P. Abbeel, K. Sreenath, “SuperMimic: Scaling Humanoid Whole-Body Control with One Differentiable World Model”, arXiv 2610.28480, CoRL 2026. Numbers come from the paper unless a box says example, illustration or general practice.

Simple but Stable, Fast and Safe

Achieve End-to-end Control by High-Fidelity Differentiable Simulation

F. Li, S. Wang, Y. Huang, F. Sun, S. Wu, Y. Yan, D. Zou, W. Yu · read the paper · code

Problem

Most drone planners treat the drone as a dot and output a path or velocity. At high speed a second controller cannot follow that plan, and the drone crashes.

Idea

One small network maps a depth image straight to motor-level commands. It is trained by pushing gradients back through a full physics simulator.

Result

390 of 400 simulated runs succeed, with the lowest jerk of all methods. The real drone flies 7.5 m/s through a forest with no real-world training.

§ I · The problem

The plan is fine for a dot, not for a drone

A point-mass model pretends the drone is a dot that can accelerate in any direction at once. A real quadrotor must first tilt, and its motors need time to spin up. This paper outputs CTBR (collective thrust and body rates: total thrust plus how fast to rotate about each of the 3 body axes). That is the low-level command sent straight to the flight controller.

The top chain has two gaps: the network trained on physics it will never fly, and a controller that must chase its output. The bottom chain removes both.

§ III-B · Training signal

Gradients flow back through the physics

BPTT (backpropagation through time) means: roll the simulator forward for a few steps, then apply the chain rule backward through every step. Because the simulator is written in PyTorch, the slope of the reward with respect to the network weights is exact inside the rollout. No critic has to guess it.

The slope of each reward runs back into the state, through one dynamics block per step, into the action, and ends at the network weights θ. The depth image is a plain input: no gradient enters it.

policy network simulator step reward, a known formula gradient forward data
Eq. 4
\[ J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma^{t}\,\class{c-rew}{R(s_t,a_t)}\Big] \]

The goal: the discounted sum of rewards over a rollout of \(T\) steps. \(\gamma\) is the discount, between 0 and 1.

Eq. 5–6
\[ \nabla_\theta J=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma^{t}\Big(\class{c-rew}{\frac{\partial R}{\partial s_t}}\,\frac{\partial s_t}{\partial\theta}\;+\;\class{c-rew}{\frac{\partial R}{\partial a_t}}\,\class{c-net}{\frac{\partial a_t}{\partial\theta}}\Big)\Big] \]

Chain rule. The reward slopes come from the reward formula. \(\class{c-net}{\partial a_t/\partial\theta}\) is ordinary backprop through the network. The hard part is \(\partial s_t/\partial\theta\).

Eq. 7
\[ \frac{\partial s_t}{\partial\theta}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\Big[\class{c-dyn}{\Big(\prod_{j=i+1}^{t}\frac{\partial s_j}{\partial s_{j-1}}\Big)\frac{\partial s_i}{\partial a_{i-1}}}\;\class{c-net}{\frac{\partial a_{i-1}}{\partial\theta}}\Big] \]

Every earlier action changed today's state. Each one is carried forward by one dynamics Jacobian (a matrix of slopes) per step.

# t = 2: two actions, two terms ∂s₂/∂θ = (∂s₂/∂a₁)(∂a₁/∂θ) + (∂s₂/∂s₁)(∂s₁/∂a₀)(∂a₀/∂θ) last action, direct first action, carried through one step
Eq. 10–11
\[ s_{t+1}=s_t+F(s_t,a_t)\,dt \qquad \class{c-dyn}{\frac{\partial s_{t+1}}{\partial s_t}=I+\frac{\partial F}{\partial s_t}\,dt} \qquad \class{c-dyn}{\frac{\partial s_{t+1}}{\partial a_t}=\frac{\partial F}{\partial a_t}\,dt} \]

One simulator step and its two Jacobians. PyTorch autograd computes them, however complex \(F\) is. The paper reports no exploding or vanishing gradients over its short horizons.

Eq. 2
for contrast
\[ \nabla_\theta J=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{T}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\;A^{\pi_\theta}(s_t,a_t)\Big] \]

What PPO uses instead. The advantage \(A\) (how much better an action was than average) is a critic's estimate from samples. With full drone physics that estimate is too noisy, and PPO never learns the task.

§ III-C · The simulator

What sits inside the dynamics block

Earlier work, point mass

The policy's acceleration command moves a dot directly. No attitude, no motors, no delay.

This paper, 6-DOF

State \(s=\{\mathbf p,\mathbf q,\mathbf v,\boldsymbol\Omega\}\): position, orientation, velocity, angular velocity. Action \(a=\{\boldsymbol\Omega^d,f^d\}\): 3 desired body rates and 1 thrust. The command passes through seven stages before the drone moves.

  1. Command delayThe command arrives \(D\) steps late, to copy the real communication delay.Eq. 18
    \[ a_t=a_{t-D} \]
  2. Body-rate PD controllerRate error becomes torque \(\boldsymbol\eta\). This copies what the flight controller does.Eq. 13
    \[ \boldsymbol\eta=\mathbf K_p(\boldsymbol\Omega^d-\boldsymbol\Omega)+\mathbf K_d(\dot{\boldsymbol\Omega}^d-\dot{\boldsymbol\Omega}) \]
  3. Thrust allocationTotal thrust and 3 torques become 4 motor thrusts. \(l\) = arm length, \(c\) = torque coefficient.Eq. 14
    \[ \begin{bmatrix}f_1\\f_2\\f_3\\f_4\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1&1\\0&l&0&-l\\-l&0&l&0\\c&-c&c&-c\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}f\\\eta_x\\\eta_y\\\eta_z\end{bmatrix} \]
  4. Motor resolvingWanted thrust becomes wanted rotor speed. \(k_0,k_1,k_2\) are measured on a thrust stand.Eq. 16
    \[ \omega_i^d=\sqrt{\frac{f_i-k_1\dot\omega_i-k_0}{k_2}} \]
  5. Motor lagA rotor cannot change speed instantly. \(\tau\) = motor time constant. Printed as in the paper; a standard first-order lag would use \(1-e^{-dt/\tau}\).Eq. 17
    \[ \omega_i\leftarrow\omega_i+(\omega_i^d-\omega_i)\,e^{-dt/\tau} \]
  6. Actual thrustReal rotor speeds give the real thrusts, then the real total force and torque (Eq. 14 backward).Eq. 15
    \[ f_i=k_2\,\omega_i^2+k_1\,\dot\omega_i+k_0 \]
  7. Rigid body, then integrateNewton and Euler equations give \(\dot s=F(s,a)\). \(\mathbf d\) = disturbance, \(\mathbf J\) = inertia, \(\otimes\) = quaternion product.Eq. 12, then Eq. 10
    \[ \dot{\mathbf p}=\mathbf v \qquad \dot{\mathbf v}=\tfrac1m\mathbf R_{WB}(\mathbf f+\mathbf d)+\mathbf g \qquad \dot{\mathbf q}=\tfrac12\,\mathbf q\otimes\boldsymbol\Omega \qquad \dot{\boldsymbol\Omega}=\mathbf J^{-1}\big(\boldsymbol\eta-\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf J\boldsymbol\Omega\big) \]
§ III-D · Reward and observation

Five terms, all with a known slope

\[ r=c_v\,r_{\mathbf v}+c_p\,r_p+c_s\,r_s+c_a\,r_a+c_c\,r_c \qquad\text{(Eq. 19)} \]
TermFormulaWhat it does
Velocity
Eq. 20		 \( r_{\mathbf v}=\mathrm{SmoothL1}(\lVert\mathbf v-\mathbf v^d\rVert,0) \) Follow the commanded velocity \(\mathbf v^d\). The Huber shape goes easy on small errors, so the drone may slow down to dodge.
Look ahead
Eq. 21		 \( r_p=\dfrac{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf x_B}{\lVert\mathbf v\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf x_B\rVert} \) Cosine between velocity and camera axis \(\mathbf x_B\). Flying where the camera points gives 1. Flying sideways gives 0.
Stability
Eq. 22		 \( r_s=\lVert\boldsymbol\Omega\rVert+c_{s2}\lVert\dot{\mathbf v}\rVert \) Spin rate plus acceleration. Keeps the flight smooth.
Keep clear
Eq. 23		 \( r_a=-\,\mathrm{clip}(d_{risk}-d_{col}-r_{uav},0)^2\cdot\mathrm{detach}(v_{appr}) \) Starts when the nearest obstacle is closer than \(d_{risk}\). Scaled by closing speed \(v_{appr}\). detach means the speed scales the penalty but gets no gradient.
Collision
Eq. 24		 \( r_c=-32\ln\!\big(e^{-7.5\,(d_{col}-r_{uav})}\big) \) Strong term near contact. As printed it equals \(240\,(d_{col}-r_{uav})\), a straight line. The code may use a softplus \(\ln(1+e^{\cdot})\); check the repo.
# "Keep clear" with example numbers (mine, not the paper's) d_risk = 1.0 m, r_uav = 0.15 m, nearest obstacle d_col = 0.5 m, closing at 4 m/s r_a = -(1.0 - 0.5 - 0.15)² × 4 = -(0.35)² × 4 = -0.49

\(r_{\mathbf v}\) and \(r_s\) are costs as written, so their weights must be negative. That is my reading; the paper does not list the weights. Only position \(\mathbf p\) carries gradient from the reward back through the simulator.

Observation. The depth image is inverted and max-pooled to \(12\times16\), so near obstacles stand out. The state part is \(\mathbf v^d_H\), \(\mathbf v_H\), \(\boldsymbol\Omega\), \(\mathbf q\): 13 numbers. Velocities are rotated into the heading frame (world frame turned by yaw \(\psi\) only), \(\mathbf v_H=\mathbf R_{WH}\mathbf v\) (Eq. 25–26).

§ III-E · Gradient field reshaping

Teach it to steer, not to brake

The distance reward pushes the drone straight away from the nearest obstacle point. This is the same push an ESDF gives (Euclidean signed distance field: a map of distance to the nearest obstacle). Head-on, that push is exactly backward, so the policy learns to brake. The fix: rotate the nearest point by an angle \(\Delta a\) before computing the reward.

Eq. 27
\[ x=\frac{\mathrm{clip}(d,d_{\min},d_{\max})-d_{\min}}{d_{\max}-d_{\min}} \qquad \tilde a=\mathrm{clip}(a,0,a_{\max}) \qquad \Delta a=(a_{\max}-\tilde a)\;\alpha\,\big[1-e^{-3(1-x)^2}\big] \]

\(d\) = distance to the nearest point. \(a\) = incidence angle between the velocity and the direction to that point (0° is head-on). The shift is largest when the obstacle is close and dead ahead. It is zero beyond \(d_{\max}\), and zero once \(a\ge a_{\max}=90°\), because then the drone is already passing.

§ III-F · Training setup

A small network and one sampling fix

All layers use LeakyReLU. No memory between frames: each command comes from the current image and state alone. Trained with SHAC across three groups of random scenes (boxes and pillars, short pillars, tall pillars).

The sampling fix. A slow flight stays in the scene longer, so it leaves more samples. If commanded speeds are drawn uniformly, the data is mostly slow flight. Drawing speeds in proportion to the speed itself cancels this.

Eq. 28–31
\[ p_{\text{data}}(v^d)\propto\frac{1}{v^d}\,p_{\text{cmd}}(v^d) \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; p_{\text{cmd}}(v^d)=\frac{2\,v^d}{v_{\max}^2-v_{\min}^2} \qquad v^d=\sqrt{v_{\min}^2+u\,(v_{\max}^2-v_{\min}^2)},\;\; u\sim\mathcal U(0,1) \]

Flight time scales as \(1/v^d\), so sample count does too. The last formula is how to draw such a speed from one uniform random number \(u\).

# example range 2 to 10 m/s (the range in both charts; mine, not the paper's) u = 0.5 → v = sqrt(4 + 0.5 × 96) = sqrt(52) = 7.2 m/s # uniform sampling would give 6.0

§ V · Limits

What to keep in mind

  • No route planning. It dodges what it sees but cannot solve a maze. The authors suggest using it as a safety layer under a planner that supplies the direction.
  • Baselines ran in their own simulators. All methods saw the same rendered scenes, but each baseline kept its native dynamics and controller stack. The comparison is fair to them, yet not one identical physics engine.
  • It still needs a state estimate. Velocity and orientation come from visual-inertial odometry on the drone.
  • Two printed formulas look off (Eq. 17 and Eq. 24, noted above). Check the released code before reimplementing.

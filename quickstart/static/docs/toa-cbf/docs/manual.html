TOA-CBF Reproduction Manual

Use this manual to set up the simulator, read the finished experiments, or train a drone policy. It covers our simulated reproduction of a paper in IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters (RA-L). The paper's method, which we call TOA-CBF, adds training losses that steer a drone around obstacles and make it brake in time. We also train it on charpi, a model of our own drone.

Liu, Yang, Li, Guo, Wang (Harbin Institute of Technology). Built on DiffPhysDrone: Zhang, Hu, Song, Zou, Lin, “Learning vision-based agile flight via differentiable physics”, Nat. Mach. Intell. 7, 954–966 (2025).

It takes the 4-D throttle + body-rate action that our real drone uses: four numbers in [−1, 1], total thrust, then roll, pitch and yaw rates. Training and simulated evaluation are done.

Not covered

No policy from this repository has flown on a physical drone. Charpi results are simulated flights, and the ONNX exports passed only numerical checks. Also not reproduced (details):

  • Flightmare, the Unity-based photorealistic simulator of the paper's tests
  • the EGO-Planner (optimization-based planner) and Agile (learned high-speed flight, Loquercio et al.) baselines
  • the F250, the authors' 250 mm quadrotor, and its real flights

Our evaluator re-creates the paper's evaluation protocol in Python: its scenes, target speeds, trial counts and success rule. A trial is one flight in one test scene at one target speed.

How the method works

This repository adds the paper's three changes to DiffPhysDrone's differentiable simulator. In that simulator every step can be differentiated, so a loss on the flight reaches the policy as a gradient. A gradient is how much the loss would change for a small change in a weight or input. The paper guide explains each equation.

  • TOA guidance: learn a route around obstacles. Each episode (one scene's flight) gets a new TOA field. JFA, the jump flooding algorithm, builds it on the GPU by copying distances over long, then shorter, jumps. The loss uses a straight-through gradient: the forward pass reads the field value, the backward pass its stored slope (Eq. (3)–(5) define the field and this loss). Only training uses the field.
  • Velocity-aligned yaw: look where the drone is going. The nose turns toward the direction of travel, so the forward depth camera looks ahead. A first-order low-pass filter smooths the turn: each step, the heading moves part of the way to its target.
  • CBF loss: brake before reaching an obstacle. The rule v_close ≤ k·h caps v_close, the speed toward the nearest obstacle along its surface normal. The cap is the CBF gain k (s⁻¹) times the clearance h: the distance to that obstacle minus a safety margin m, in meters. With the default k = 2.5 s⁻¹ (--cbf_k), 1.2 m of clearance allows up to 3 m/s. A softplus penalty, a smooth max(0, x), teaches the rule (the paper's Eq. (6)–(7) and Algorithm 1).

After training, the policy needs only the depth image, velocity, goal direction, attitude and the margin m, which is sampled per training episode. It uses no map and no solver.

Reset64 random scenes: spheres, vertical and horizontal cylinders, boxes
TOA fieldgrid ≤ 128³ cells of 0.2 m (larger for long routes), JFA from the goal, slope from neighbor cells (central differences)
Rollout150 steps at 15 Hz: ray-cast depth (one ray per pixel) → CNN-GRU policy → acceleration command (Eq. (1) control law) → dynamics
LossesLv, Ltoa, Lcbf, Lcollide, Lacc, Ljerk: velocity tracking, TOA, CBF, collision, acceleration, jerk (sum in Eq. (8), weights in Table I)

The default plant is the point mass. --dynamics charpi trains on charpi instead. A 100 Hz attitude controller then turns each acceleration command into the throttle + body-rate action. The policy and losses stay the same; the charpi training section explains the new dynamics.

    python -m pytest -q tests

    Expect 41 passed on the GPU machine. On 2026-10-01 the suite took 16.6 s with OMP_NUM_THREADS=4 MKL_NUM_THREADS=4, ROS's PYTHONPATH cleared and the Inductor cache warm (Inductor: torch.compile's code generator, which keeps compiled kernels on disk).

    Without a GPU, the two GPU-only parity tests are skipped. A parity test checks that two versions give matching numbers: here CUDA vs. fallback, and the compiled vs. eager charpi loop. The first, test_matches_cuda_extension, checks each primitive family separately and compares relative differences. At most 8 of 98,304 pixels may differ by more than 0.1 % (0–2 measured), nearest points by at most 0.0001 m.

    Why the depth parity test changed

    The original GPU suite failed 1 of 19 tests: its absolute 0.001 m depth tolerance was too strict. Rays that graze the ground can hit 100,000 m or more away, where float32 round-off alone exceeds it. They also made an older check line, max |cuda - torch| = 3.59e+03 m, look alarming. The test and the check line now compare relative differences.

    A later review found that a module alias from toanav/backend.py made the full suite compare the fallback with itself. Fixed: the test now asserts that it uses the real extension.

The paper-literal JFA uses the paper’s propagation rule, Eq. (3)–(4), exactly as printed. Its TOA field is about 10× cheaper (0.09 vs. 0.90 s), so a whole iteration runs about 4× faster, but it is much less accurate (why).

Once compiled, charpi adds little (1.09 vs. 1.08 s/it), because the TOA field dominates. Compiling the 100 Hz loop on the first iteration takes about a minute, or seconds with a warm Inductor on-disk cache. So the first log line, after 25 iterations, may show about 3.2 s/it: a one-time compile, not a hang.

Why compiling matters: compiled and eager rollout timings

One 150-step rollout plus backward pass (B = 64): compiled charpi 0.105 s, eager charpi 3.6 s, plain env.run 0.015 s. Uncompiled, kernel launches dominate, adding about 4 s per rollout.

Expect slower acceleration than the point mass

Charpi’s acceleration response is about 2.4× slower than the paper’s point mass: fitted lag 5 s⁻¹ against 12 s⁻¹ (higher = faster; why). Its limits, with formulas in export:

  • Hover throttle is about 0.26. Thrust spans 10.4–39.6 N, or 0.83–3.15 g of specific force (thrust divided by mass).
  • In level flight, downward acceleration is at most about 1.7 m/s². At the 0.15 throttle floor, thrust still gives 8.1 m/s² upward against gravity’s 9.81 m/s².
  • Pitch rate is at most 2.5 rad/s; angular acceleration at most 50–60 rad/s².

Early training is slower too. After 300 full-size iterations on the RTX 5080, charpi reached training success 0.84 at 1.8 m/s mean speed. toa_cbf on the point mass reached 0.92 at 3.9 m/s. Do not draw conclusions from these early checks; read the local results first.

09Where this code differs from the paper

Where the paper leaves a detail open, or a printed equation misbehaves, this code makes its own choice. Flags restore the paper’s or DiffPhysDrone’s behavior where noted. Read these before comparing results with the paper or changing loss weights.

  • TOA field: the default JFA (jfa_safe) checks for walls and stays within 0.2–0.3 % of BFS on average. The paper-literal JFA builds the field about 10× faster but much less accurately.
  • CBF loss: constants chosen here; the ground counts as an obstacle.
  • Evaluation: a Python re-creation of the paper’s Flightmare tests, with simpler obstacles and depth sensor.
  • Charpi: a simulated model of our own drone, outside the paper’s reproduction.
Default JFA versus the paper-literal JFA

The paper-literal JFA applies Eq. (3), the paper’s JFA update, as printed. Its TOA field is about 10× cheaper to compute (run times), but it can leak through thin walls.

The default JFA (jfa_safe) splits each jump into axis-aligned legs and accepts a leg only if every voxel on it is free, so it never crosses a wall. A step to a neighbor voxel costs δ, √2δ or √3δ (octile costs). Three passes plus 8 cleanups take 29–32 iterations; BFS needs about 130.

Eq. (3) takes the minimum over a dilated 3×3×3 window (neighbors k voxels apart) and adds k·δ. Here k is the jump length in voxels, not the CBF gain. Diagonal steps then cost the same as axis steps (Chebyshev metric). At δ = 0.2 m, a diagonal step in a plane costs 0.2 m, not about 0.28 m. So ∇Φ points along the dominant axis. A k-voxel jump can also skip obstacles thinner than k voxels: in a U-shaped trap the start distance is over 20 % too short. Cleanups only lower values, so they cannot repair such a leak.

How the grid covers long routes and inflates obstacles

Voxels grow beyond δ = 0.2 m on long routes. At 0.2 m, at most 128 voxels per axis span only 25.6 m, but DiffPhysDrone’s high-speed training scenes put start and goal up to about 120 m apart. Over 24 scenes at the default --speed_mtp 4, the median voxel is 0.58 m, the largest 0.9 m. At --speed_mtp 1 it stays 0.2 m.

So obstacles are inflated by 0.2 m (one voxel at the paper’s δ), not one voxel of any size. At the coarse voxel sizes, one-voxel inflation cut the start off from the goal in 12 % of those scenes; 0.2 m inflation, in 4 %. A voxel is occupied within half a voxel of a primitive. The grid bottom sits half a voxel above the ground, and the grid edge acts as a wall.

CBF constants chosen here, and how to tune braking

The paper gives no values for k, β, dact or M. The defaults are k = 2.5 s⁻¹, β = 2, dact = 1.5 m and M = 10 sub-positions (DiffPhysDrone’s sub_div). Clearance is h = d − m, with d the exact distance to the nearest surface minus the drone’s radius. The loss penalizes closing faster than k·h (worked example).

If the policy is too timid at 9 m/s, raise --cbf_k or lower --coef_cbf. If it clips obstacles, do the opposite.

Why the ground keeps the CBF active

The ground counts as an obstacle, so cbf_active is about 0.87 in training. The losses’ obstacle set (toanav/geometry.scene_sdf) holds the ground plane z = −1 m beside the primitives, and training starts 0.4–1.5 m above it. With dact = 1.5 m, every point on the start → goal lines is within the activation distance (69 % without the ground). The CBF also caps descent speed near the ground at k·h.

The paper does not say whether its obstacle set includes the ground. This is an observation only: no code change, no flag.

Why the losses use exact signed distances

DiffPhysDrone’s find_nearest_pt gives the right direction but a wrong distance for spheres and cylinders (o + dist·(c − o), not normalized). The losses here use exact signed distances from toanav/geometry.py, with a straight-through gradient along the surface normal. The PyTorch fallback copies the original behavior, wrong distance included, so the DiffPhys baseline is unaffected.

Collision loss: speed floor and clearance margin

The collision loss, Eq. (9), follows DiffPhysDrone: sharpness α = 32 (not Eq. (2)’s α), and approach speed from the M sub-positions, floored at 1 m/s instead of 0. So a drone near a wall is penalized even while moving away. --collide_vmin 0 gives the strict (·)⁺, the positive part max(0, ·).

The clearance margin m is also subtracted from d (--no-collide_use_margin turns this off). Without it, m would do nothing in the Distance and TOA variants, though the paper says all variants share one observation.

Paper action output versus DiffPhysDrone’s

The paper’s control law, Eq. (1), uses the measured velocity vt and a 3-D output. DiffPhysDrone instead predicts a velocity with a second head. The default follows the paper; --action_mode diffphys restores DiffPhysDrone’s version.

Goal guidance, scene randomization and wind

Lv and the observed target velocity keep the straight-line (Euclidean) goal direction, as the paper’s loss section (Sec. III-D) says. The geodesic distance enters only through Ltoa.

Scenes and randomization are DiffPhysDrone’s single-agent settings: max speed 3–13 m/s, ground hills, random yaw rotation, yaw drift, camera pitch 20°, FOV tan 0.82. The paper lists these but gives no values. Wind is an OU process with a 2 s time constant, not a constant per episode.

What the local evaluation does and does not reproduce

The local evaluation re-creates the paper’s protocol in Python, without:

  • Flightmare/Unity or the Hummingbird model;
  • SGM stereo from a 640×480 camera at 15 Hz (a stereo error model stands in);
  • the EGO-Planner and Agile baselines;
  • the F250 quadrotor, or any real flight.

Trees are bare cylinders, without branches or foliage. Large-obstacle sizes are our guesses from Fig. 4(a), the paper’s trajectory plot in the large-obstacle corridor. Evaluation uses nominal dynamics (on the charpi plant: DR off), collision radius 0.15 m and clearance margin m = 0.2 m.

Choices for the charpi simulation

Charpi goes beyond the paper. The paper’s point mass follows an acceleration command through a first-order lag. Charpi takes throttle + body-rate commands, so an attitude controller sits between policy and plant. The plant flies the drone; a simpler surrogate supplies the gradient (how training uses them).

Charpi dynamics, delays, limits and reference checks

toanav/charpi.py is a line-by-line PyTorch port of charpi_physics.py. It models:

  • a 2-step (20 ms) command delay;
  • a per-axis 2nd-order closed-loop rate response, fitted to real CTBR flight logs (real) or PX4-SITL (sitl);
  • the thrust curve with 30 ms motor lag;
  • rotor inflow (air through the rotors, which changes thrust as airspeed changes) and lateral drag;
  • 1.279 kg mass, a 10 ms control step and a 2 ms physics step.

Attitude controller and thrust calculation

The controller is PX4’s tilt-prioritized attitude controller (AttitudeControl::update + bodyzToAttitude), in ENU / FLU with [x, y, z, w] quaternions. It uses PX4’s default MC_ROLL_P, MC_PITCH_P, MC_YAW_P and MC_YAW_WEIGHT (values in the charpi flag table).

Thrust points along u + g, tilted at most 60°. Collective thrust is the larger of PX4’s projection and a vertical-hold floor (formula). Projection alone sank 0.1–0.9 m per maneuver while the attitude loop settles (0.3–0.45 s), and PX4’s mass·|f| climbs as much. The floor holds the height.

Throttle comes from inverting the nominal thrust curve. Like the real vehicle, the controller does not know the randomized thrust scale.

How the surrogate is fitted and what it receives

The surrogate is DiffPhysDrone’s env.run point mass, used straight-through as in ctbr_dynamics_pm of qpg-quad-jax. Its lag is 5 s⁻¹ (10 % standard deviation per episode), not DiffPhysDrone’s 12 s⁻¹ (standard deviation 1.2): charpi responds about 2.4× slower. It also takes charpi’s lateral drag at hover (about 0.24 s⁻¹) and no z drag.

scripts/charpi/fit_charpi_surrogate.py fitted the lag on random 15 Hz commands flown through controller and plant. For the real fit, lag 5.0 leaves an RMS error of 1.66 m/s² on an RMS acceleration of 4.78 m/s². That explains about 88 % of the variance. Lag 12 leaves 2.80 m/s². Other best fits: 5.0 with the narrow DR band, 4.0 for the sitl fit, 3.5 with twice as aggressive commands.

The surrogate gets charpi.feasible_accel(u): u clamped to the tilt limit and to what controller and nominal plant deliver in level flight. That is fz from 8.1 to 30.9 m/s², or uz from −1.70 to +21.1 m/s². Command parts the plant ignores get zero gradient; the plant itself gets the raw u.

Heading lag, episode start and command timing

The heading setpoint comes from DiffPhysDrone’s yaw filter, whose state (its own virtual attitude) persists across steps. So the plant is asked to turn exactly as DiffPhysDrone would; its nose trails by about the yaw loop’s time constant, 1/MC_YAW_P ≈ 0.36 s.

Episodes use a trimmed start: steady flight for the current command, not rest, with lagged thrust and delay buffer pre-filled. Charpi’s zero state would drop a flying vehicle for 30 ms.

DiffPhysDrone’s rollout latency is kept: a command acts one policy step later, plus charpi’s 20 ms command delay. A 15 Hz step is 6 or 7 ticks of 10 ms; step-time jitter (random step lengths in training) makes it 3–10.

A CUDA-graph pitfall in the compiled charpi loop

A tensor created or cached inside the compiled region and kept past one call can be overwritten by a later CUDA-graph replay. A constant-tensor cache filled there once broke this way; the fix is in toanav/charpi.py. The error reads accessing tensor output of CUDAGraphs that has been overwritten by a subsequent run.

If the error returns after a local change, use --no-charpi_compile while debugging. Look in _charpi_ticks_masked for tensors that outlive one call. Build them outside the compiled region, or as graph constants as _vec does.

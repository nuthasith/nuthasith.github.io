Primer for newcomers · vision-based drone racing

SkyDreamer, Dreamer world models, and racing drones from pixels

Four parts, read in order. Part 4 compares it with SuperMimic. The diagrams carry most of the explanation, and every equation has a worked number next to it.

The paper

SkyDreamer flies a racing drone through gates using a 64×64 gate mask from a cheap camera, the gyro and the motor speeds, and writes the four motor commands itself. It is trained in simulation with a Dreamer world model and runs entirely onboard: 75 counted real laps without a crash, up to 21 m/s and 6 g.

World model

A recurrent network that remembers what it has seen and predicts what comes next. In training it must also spell out the drone's true position, speed and physical parameters, so in flight it doubles as a state estimator you can read.

Vs SuperMimic

SuperMimic's world model is a fast, differentiable simulator that stays in the lab. SkyDreamer's world model flies on the drone as its memory and state estimate. Part 4 has the full comparison.

Part 1 · Basics

How a racing drone flies and learns

Reinforcement learning (RL): a policy picks actions, the world answers, and a reward says how well it went. Training adjusts the policy's weights to raise the total reward. In drone racing, faster laps through every gate earn more reward.

The policy and what it maximizes
\[ u_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid o_{\le t}) \qquad\qquad J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t\ge 0} \gamma^t\, r_t\Big] \]
  • \(o_t\) observationgate mask, gyro, motor speeds at step \(t\)
  • \(o_{\le t}\) historyall observations so far; one frame is not enough
  • \(u_t\) actionfour motor commands, each in [0, 1]
  • \(r_t\) rewardthe score for that one step
  • \(\pi_\theta\) policya neural network with weights \(\theta\)
  • \(\gamma\) discounthow much later rewards count (0.997)
With \(\gamma = 0.997\), a reward 300 steps (3.3 s) ahead counts \(0.997^{300} \approx 0.41\) as much as one now. That is about one lap on the paper's small tracks, so rewards a full lap ahead still matter.

How a quadrotor makes force

Each of the four propellers pushes along the drone's up axis with a force that grows with the square of its speed. To move sideways, the drone tilts so part of that push points where it wants to go. Spinning some propellers faster than others makes it rotate.

Thrust and motor response · the paper's model
\[ T = k_\omega \sum_{i=1}^{4}\omega_i^2 \qquad \dot\omega_i = \frac{\omega_{c,i}-\omega_i}{\tau} \qquad \omega_{c,i} = (\omega_{\max}-\omega_{\min})\sqrt{k\,u_i^2+(1-k)\,u_i}\;+\;\omega_{\min} \]

\(T\) is thrust per kilogram (an acceleration) at zero airspeed. \(\omega_{c,i}\) is the speed motor \(i\) heads toward for command \(u_i\); \(\tau\) is how fast it gets there. Paper values: \(k_\omega = 1.55\times10^{-6}\), \(\omega_{\min} = 342\) rad/s, \(\omega_{\max} = 3{,}100\) rad/s, \(k = 0.5\), \(\tau = 0.03\) s.

Hover needs \(4\,k_\omega\,\omega^2 = 9.81\), so \(\omega \approx 1{,}260\) rad/s per motor. Command \(u = 0.5\) gives \(\omega_c = 2{,}758\cdot\sqrt{0.375} + 342 \approx 2{,}030\) rad/s. Starting from hover, one 11.1 ms step closes \(1 - e^{-11.1/30} \approx 31\%\) of the gap, so the motor reaches about 1,500 rad/s.
The simulator · NED axes, z points down
\[ \dot p = v \qquad \dot q = \tfrac12\, q \otimes [\,0,\ \Omega\,] \qquad \dot v = g\,e_3 + R(q)\,F + \varepsilon_a \qquad \dot\Omega = M + \varepsilon_M \]

\(q\) = attitude quaternion, \(\Omega\) = body rates (spin about the drone's own axes), \(R\) = body-to-world rotation. \(F\) adds thrust and drag; \(M\) holds the moments from the four motors plus gyroscopic terms. \(\varepsilon\) are random pushes for robustness. Written in JAX and stepped with fourth-order Runge–Kutta every 2.2 ms, about 5 sim steps per policy step.

Who turns commands into motor power?

Most racing policies output CTBR (collective thrust and body rates): one total thrust and three spin rates. A fast, hand-tuned controller (PID or INDI) then works out the motor commands. SkyDreamer skips that layer, following the guidance-and-control networks (G&CNETs) introduced by ESA and earlier TU Delft work.

Why bother: a motor is simpler to model than a tuned controller, so the simulator matches reality better, and the policy gets full use of the motors. The cost: the policy must learn to stabilize the drone too, and a careless one can burn out motors (Part 2 shows the fix).

The racing reward

The reward
\[ r_t = 5\,r_{\text{prog}} - r_{\text{rate}} + 30\,r_{\text{gate}} \] \[ r_{\text{prog}} = \lVert p_{t-1,g}\rVert - \lVert p_{t,g}\rVert \qquad r_{\text{gate}} = 1 - \frac{\max(|y_{t,g}|,\,|z_{t,g}|)}{d_g}\ \ \text{(on crossing, else 0)} \qquad r_{\text{rate}} = \frac{e^{\min(\lVert\Omega_t\rVert_1,\,17)} - 1}{2 f_c \cdot 10^5} \]

\(p_{t,g}\) = drone position relative to the next gate; \(y, z\) = sideways and vertical offset at the crossing. \(d_g = 0.8\) m. \(\lVert\Omega_t\rVert_1\) = sum of the absolute spin rates (rad/s). \(f_c = 90\) Hz. The rate penalty limits spinning, which would saturate the gyro and blur the camera.

At 15 m/s the drone closes 0.17 m per step, so progress pays \(5 \times 0.17 = 0.83\) per step. Crossing 0.2 m off center pays \(30 \times 0.75 = 22.5\). The rate penalty is a wall: \(\lVert\Omega\rVert_1 = 10\) rad/s costs 0.001 per step, 15 rad/s costs 0.18, and at the 17 rad/s cap it costs 1.34, more than the progress reward.

The progress reward does not prescribe a path: summed over one approach, it equals the starting distance whatever route is flown. The discount is what makes arriving sooner worth more.

When an episode ends:

  • Gate crash: crossing a gate, or its pre- or post-gate, more than \(d_g\) from the center.
  • Ground crash: near the floor (below 0.5 m) while sinking faster than 1 m/s or tilted past 60°. The real drone starts on a raised podium, so it learns to climb before speeding off.
  • After a crash, all future reward is zero. That loss is the only crash penalty; an extra collision penalty slowed learning.
  • There is no reward for looking at gates. The policy learns to point its camera at them anyway.
Part 2 · World models

A world model that remembers

If you read the SuperMimic primer: its world model predicted the next full state from the current one. That works when the robot knows its state. A racing drone does not. It sees a small, rough gate mask and must work out its position and speed over time.

Formally this is a POMDP (partially observable Markov decision process): the true state \(s_t\) exists, but the agent only gets observations \(o_t\). A good policy therefore needs memory.

Why one frame is not enough. At 64×64 the camera's focal length is 25 px, so a 1.5 m gate 5 m away is \(25 \times 1.5 / 5 = 7.5\) px wide. At 15 m/s the drone covers 0.17 m in one frame, and the gate grows to 7.76 px. The change, 0.26 px, is less than one pixel. Speed has to be pieced together over many frames, together with gyro and motor readings.

Dreamer's world model: a memory plus a code

DreamerV3 (Hafner et al.) is the most widely used world-model RL method. Its model, the RSSM (recurrent state-space model), keeps two things: \(h_t\), a memory vector updated by a GRU (a small recurrent network), and \(z_t\), a discrete code that summarizes the latest observation. Together they form the model state \(s_t = (h_t, z_t)\).

The RSSM · six small networks
\[ h_t = f_\phi(h_{t-1},\, z_{t-1},\, u_{t-1}) \qquad z_t \sim q_\phi(z_t \mid h_t,\, o_t) \qquad \hat z_t \sim p_\phi(\hat z_t \mid h_t) \] \[ \hat\imath_t \sim p_\phi(i_t \mid h_t, z_t) \qquad \hat r_t \sim p_\phi(r_t \mid h_t, z_t) \qquad \hat c_t \sim p_\phi(c_t \mid h_t, z_t) \]

\(\phi\) = world-model weights (the paper writes \(\theta\) for everything; \(\phi\) keeps it apart from the policy). \(q\) is the encoder and sees the observation; \(p\) is the dynamics predictor and does not. \(i_t\) is the decoder's target, explained below. \(c_t = 1\) while the episode goes on and 0 after a crash.

The code \(z\) is a set of one-hot slots. With 2 slots of 4 choices, \(z = [0,1,0,0 \mid 0,0,0,1]\) is one of 16 possible codes. DreamerV2 used 32 slots of 32 choices. A discrete code lets the model say “either this or that” instead of averaging two possible futures into a blur.
World-model loss · DreamerV3
\[ \mathcal L(\phi) = \mathbb E\sum_t\Big[\underbrace{-\ln p_\phi(i_t\mid s_t) - \ln p_\phi(r_t\mid s_t) - \ln p_\phi(c_t\mid s_t)}_{\text{prediction}} + \beta_{\text{dyn}}\underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\big[\mathrm{sg}(q_\phi)\,\Vert\,p_\phi\big]}_{\text{teach the predictor}} + \beta_{\text{rep}}\underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\big[q_\phi\,\Vert\,\mathrm{sg}(p_\phi)\big]}_{\text{keep codes guessable}}\Big] \]

sg = stop-gradient (treat as a constant). KL (Kullback–Leibler divergence) measures how far the predictor's guess is from what the encoder saw. DreamerV3 stops pushing once the KL is below 1 nat (a floor called free bits) and weights the second KL lightly (\(\beta_{\text{rep}} = 0.1\)). SkyDreamer trains on replayed sequences of 64 steps, later 256.

One slot, two choices. Before the image, the predictor guesses [0.6, 0.4]. After the image, the encoder says [0.95, 0.05]. \(\mathrm{KL} = 0.95\ln\frac{0.95}{0.6} + 0.05\ln\frac{0.05}{0.4} \approx 0.33\) nats. If that were the whole code, it would sit under the 1-nat floor, and the loss would leave it alone.

SkyDreamer's twist: decode the true state, not the picture

Standard Dreamer trains the decoder to rebuild the observation, which for images is often a blur. Informed Dreamer (Lambrechts et al., 2024) decodes privileged information instead: things the simulator knows but the drone never sees. SkyDreamer decodes the full state and the drone's physical parameters. The image itself is left out of the target.

Informed POMDP · what the decoder must rebuild
\[ i_t = \{\,o_t,\ o_t^+\,\} \setminus \{X\}, \qquad o_t^+ = [\,p_w,\ p_g,\ v_w,\ v_g,\ \lambda,\ \Omega,\ \omega,\ c_e,\ d\,] \] \[ \pi_\theta^* = \arg\max_{\pi_\theta}\ \mathbb E\Big[\sum_t \gamma^t R(s_t, u_t)\Big], \qquad u_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid o_{\le t},\, u_{\lt t}) \]

\(X\) = the gate mask; \(o^+\) = privileged observations, available in training only. The policy never takes \(o^+\) as input. It benefits only because the latent it reads has been forced to carry that information.

What this buys:

  • A readable estimator. In flight you can run the decoder and plot where the model thinks the drone is. In simulation its position estimates sit within 10–15 cm of the truth.
  • Offline debugging. Log a real flight, change the world model, then replay the same observations through it to check a fix, with no new flight.
  • A built-in parameter estimator. Because \(d\) is a target, the model learns to infer thrust gain and motor limits from how the drone responds (Part 3).

SkyDreamer does not run its own comparison against plain DreamerV3. The claim that decoding privileged information speeds up learning comes from the informed-Dreamer paper.

Training the actor inside the dream

Training cycles through three phases, as in SuperMimic: collect simulator data into a replay buffer, fit the world model on replayed sequences, then train the actor and critic only on imagined rollouts. Each dream starts from a real model state and runs 16 steps ahead.

λ-return · the critic's target
\[ R^\lambda_t = \hat r_{t+1} + \gamma\,\hat c_{t+1}\Big[(1-\lambda)\,v_\psi(s_{t+1}) + \lambda\,R^\lambda_{t+1}\Big], \qquad R^\lambda_H = v_\psi(s_H) \]

\(H = 16\) imagined steps, \(\gamma = 0.997\), \(\lambda = 0.95\) (DreamerV3 default). The critic \(v_\psi\) is trained to match \(R^\lambda_t\). Same idea as SuperMimic's Eq. 3.

Actor loss · REINFORCE + entropy + smoothness
\[ \mathcal L(\theta) = -\sum_t \mathrm{sg}\!\left(\frac{R^\lambda_t - v_\psi(s_t)}{\max(1,\,S)}\right)\ln\pi_\theta(u_t\mid s_t)\ -\ \eta\,\mathrm H\big[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t)\big]\ +\ \lambda_{\text{smooth}}\,\mathbb E_t\,\lVert\mu_t-\mu_{t-1}\rVert_2^2 \]

\(s_t = (h_t, z_t)\). \(S\) = spread of recent returns (95th minus 5th percentile), so the step size does not depend on the reward's scale. \(\mathrm H\) = entropy, which keeps some exploration noise; \(\eta = 3\cdot10^{-4}\), later \(1\cdot10^{-5}\). \(\mu_t\) = the policy's mean motor command; \(\lambda_{\text{smooth}} = 0.002\).

A dreamed action earned a λ-return of 12.0 where the critic expected 10.0, with return spread \(S = 20\). Its weight is \((12 - 10)/20 = 0.1\), so its probability is nudged up. A return of 8.0 would give −0.1 and nudge it down. Nothing is differentiated through the world model.

Why the smoothness term. Without it, policies drifted toward bang-bang control: motors fully on or fully off. That is fine in simulation and burns motors in reality. The penalty touches only the mean, so it does not fight the entropy term, and in flight the drone uses the mean alone, with no noise.

Part 3 · The paper

Earlier end-to-end vision policies ran offboard, used motion capture to render their images in real flights, and were capped well below champion speeds. Idea. Closing the vision gap

Rendered masks are perfect. GateNet's real masks are thinner, blotchy, sometimes miss pieces and sometimes mark things that are not gates. The paper roughens the rendered masks in three ways before the world model sees them.

One standard camera, and the rolling-shutter warp
\[ K = \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac{25}{64}W & 0 & 0.5W \\ 0 & \tfrac{25}{64}H & 0.5H \\ 0&0&1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -s\,r_c & \tfrac{W}{2}\,s\,r_c \\ 0 & 1+s\,q_c & -\tfrac{H}{2}\,s\,q_c \end{bmatrix}, \quad s \in [0,\ 0.02] \]

Every drone's image is undistorted onto the same pinhole camera \(K\) (intrinsics), so the policy never needs to know the lens. How the camera is mounted (extrinsics) is not calibrated: it is randomized in training and estimated in flight. \(A\) mimics a rolling shutter, which reads rows one after another; \(r_c\), \(q_c\) = yaw and pitch rate in the camera frame.

At 64×64 the focal length is 25 px, a field of view of \(2\arctan(32/25) \approx 104°\). With \(s = 0.01\) and a yaw rate of 5 rad/s, the shear is 0.05: the top row shifts +1.6 px and the bottom row −1.6 px. A pitch rate of 5 rad/s stretches the image 5% vertically.

Flight plan: telling look-alike views apart

On the test tracks the drone sees nearly the same picture at different points of a lap, but must do different things. A memory alone can lose track. SkyDreamer adds a flight plan input that lists the next gates, and uses its own decoded position to know when to move down the list.

Flight plan vector and gate counter
\[ f_i = \big[\,p_{g_i}-p_{g_{i-1}},\ \psi_{g_i}-\psi_{g_{i-1}},\ \dots,\ \psi_{g_{i+2}}-\psi_{g_{i+1}},\ p_{g_i},\ \psi_{g_i},\ \dots,\ \psi_{g_{i+2}}\,\big]^\top \qquad i \leftarrow i+1\ \ \text{when}\ \ \hat x_g > -0.15\ \text{m} \]

\(p_{g_i}\), \(\psi_{g_i}\) = position and yaw of gate \(i\) in the world frame. The vector says nothing about where the drone is; the drone must work that out. \(\hat x_g\) = decoded distance past the current gate plane (negative means before it). In training the decoder is not trusted yet, so \(i\) advances at a random point between pre- and post-gate.

The first rule, \(\hat x_g > 0\), never fired on one real ladder flight because of a small estimation error, so that flight was aborted. The threshold was moved to −0.15 m, and that flight is excluded from the lap counts below.

Training takes about 50 hours, and each model is trained for one known track.

Part 4 · Comparison

SkyDreamer vs SuperMimic

Both learn a world model in simulation and train a policy inside it. They use the model for opposite jobs.

SuperMimic

The world model is a learned simulator: fast, smooth and differentiable, so the policy learns from exact gradients through it. It never leaves the lab; the robot runs the policy alone.

SkyDreamer

The world model is a learned memory and state estimator: it turns a stream of poor images into a belief about where the drone is and how it behaves. It flies on the drone; the policy reads its latent.

Why each design fits its problem

Humanoid tracking has a mostly measured state, so the world model needs no memory, and the bottleneck is training cost across 129,514 clips. A smooth MLP gives exact gradients, so every imagined sample teaches more than a scored trial would.

Vision racing has a hidden state and poor images, so the world model has to be a filter: something that accumulates evidence over time. Decoding the true state forces it to be a good filter and gives a readout for free. REINFORCE never needs the model's gradients, so it tolerates a model that is only roughly right and has discrete, random codes.

Shared by both

  • Replay buffer; a loop of collect → fit model → train in imagination
  • Short dreams (16 or 32 steps) plus a critic with λ-returns (λ = 0.95)
  • Dreams start from real states in the buffer
  • Reward and fall (continue) heads so dreams can be scored
  • Privileged simulator info used only in training
  • Domain randomization for sim-to-real

Borrow for a humanoid · untested ideas

  • A recurrent estimator with a privileged decoder for what the G1 measures poorly (base velocity, foot friction, payload), readable on hardware
  • Track slow drifts outside the training range, like SkyDreamer's 30% motor-speed drop: battery sag or hot motors losing torque
  • Replay logged real runs through an updated estimator to debug sim-to-real without new hardware runs
  • Smooth only the policy mean when entropy keeps exploration alive
